# Modelling conventions - shape language for every authored model

2026-09-19. The per-model half of
`2026-09-12-environment-art-direction-design.md`, which keeps the palette, the lighting and
the ground. That spec had no section on how to *shape* a model, and neither did anything
else in the repository: the model SPECs in `C:\repos\AirportMgr2Models` are dimensional
(`tug1/SPEC.md` is Goldhofer's own datasheet at +/-3%) and say nothing about craft. The
word "bevel" appeared in zero documents before this one, while eight models had already
been built. This is that gap closed, late.

Scope: aircraft, ground vehicles, buildings, props and clutter. Not the procedural surfaces
- roads, taxiways, aprons and stands are generated by `Build/`, and their edge treatment is
already ruled separately (kerbs for roads, run-offs for taxiways).

## 1. The rule

**Almost everything gets a small bevel. No infinitely sharp 90 degree edges.**

A hangar is still essentially a box; its corners are generously chamfered. A vehicle has
chunky wheel arches and simplified wheels. Roof equipment is recognisable boxes and
cylinders, not modelled machinery.

> simple geometry + bevel + good silhouette + excellent lighting

and explicitly **not**

> simple geometry + completely flat faces

because the second is what reads as *obviously low-poly*. The difference between the two is
almost entirely the bevel: a flat face meeting another flat face at a sharp right angle
gives the renderer one value on each side and nothing in between, so the edge is a colour
step. A 2-4 cm chamfer gives it a narrow band angled at the sky, which catches a highlight
under exactly the soft-penumbra sun the environment spec buys (`LightSourceAngle` 1.5 deg)
and separates form from form without any texture, any normal map or any outline pass.

**This is a reversal**, and the thing it reverses is recorded rather than quietly deleted:
the environment spec used to read *"low-poly hard-edged forms"* and *"the hard edges come
from silhouettes, not from light"*. Silhouette still does most of the work. It no longer
does all of it.

### 1.1 Widths

Starting values, in real-world units, to be judged on screen rather than defended:

| Scale of object | Bevel | Segments |
|---|---|---|
| Vehicle panel, prop, sign | 1.5 - 3 cm | 2 |
| Building corner, hangar, container | 5 - 15 cm | 2 - 3 |
| Aircraft fuselage panel joins | leave to the source mesh | - |

Two segments is the default. Three only where the object is large enough to be seen close
at the 6 m camera minimum (`BuildCameraRig.h:40-49`). One segment is a chamfer rather than
a bevel and is fine on anything that will never be near the camera.

**The width is what matters, not the polygon count.** A bevel too small to span a pixel at
150 m has cost polygons and bought nothing; a bevel wide enough to see at 600 m has rounded
the object into a pebble. The test is the same three-distance screenshot the environment
spec already mandates - 20 m, 150 m, 600 m - and the pass condition is that the object
reads crisp at 150 m and the bevel is *felt* rather than *seen*.

### 1.2 Exemptions

- **Imported real-world meshes.** The fleet arrives as `.glb` from real aircraft models;
  their edges are whatever the source gave. Do not chase them.
- **Anything thinner than its own bevel.** Fences, antennae, cables, decals, ground
  markings. A bevel on a 2 cm wire is 100% of the wire.
- **Surfaces owned by `Build/`.** Road, taxiway, apron and stand geometry is solved, and
  its edges are a separate ruling.
- **Sub-pixel clutter.** Anything that only ever appears past ~200 m.

### 1.3 Existing models are not retrofitted

`piperMeridian`, `plane2`, `plane3`, `tug1`, `utility1`, `gpu1`, `fueltruck1` and
`baggageCart1` predate this rule. They get bevels **the next time each is opened for
another reason**, not in a sweep.

The reason is cost against benefit, stated plainly: re-exporting a rigged `.blend` is not
free - reimport has already cost this project a stranded mesh and a stale reference pose -
and the benefit is an edge highlight. A model already on screen and readable is not a
defect. The next model built to the rule is worth more than eight rebuilt to it.

## 2. Proportions stay real

The diorama reading depends on it. A display model is convincing precisely because the
proportions are right and only the *scale* is wrong; a model with invented proportions is
a toy. So:

- Dimensions come from datasheets and POH figures, as they already do. Keep the citation
  in the model's `SPEC.md`, with its tolerance.
- **Vehicles look miniature through treatment, not through distortion.** Chunky arches,
  simplified wheels, softened corners, clean flat paint. Not a shortened wheelbase or an
  oversized cab.
- Field lengths compress to 0.6x (environment spec section 2.2); **aircraft, aprons,
  stands and vehicles do not.** The compression is a sim-time decision that happens to
  serve the diorama; it is not licence to scale objects.

## 3. Materials

- Mostly matte. Roughness high and constant per material; metallic zero except where the
  thing is actually bare metal.
- Flat base colours from the palette (environment spec section 1.1). No normal maps and no
  albedo texturing on authored props - the form does the work.
- Colour discipline is the palette's, not this document's: muted world, saturated
  equipment, and yellow / red / lighting colours reserved for meaning.
- One master material with instances per model family, as the fleet already does
  (`M_Fleet`, values scraped from the `.glb`). A colour typed at a second call site is a
  second source of truth.

## 4. Verification

The claim "it looks right" needs an image, the same as everywhere else in this project.

1. Import, place in the model yard (`Tools/Python/build_model_yard.py`).
2. `python Tools/Mcp.py shot` at the three distances.
3. Ask of the images, not of the mesh: is the silhouette readable at 600 m? Does the bevel
   catch a highlight at 20 m? Is it invisible at 150 m (too small) or obvious (too wide)?

**Unverified as of writing:** every number in section 1.1. They are starting points chosen
to be in the right order of magnitude, not findings. The first model built under this rule
is what turns them into figures, and this document is updated with what that model measured
rather than with what it was expected to measure.
